# RATIÉ CH. 1 — THE BUDDHIST CRITIQUE OF THE SELF
*Per-chapter analysis for the thesis: recognition as the felt re-cognition of the self.
Source lines 2548–5925. THE HARDEST-RIVAL CHAPTER (our Stage 1 test).*

---

## 1. THE CHAPTER'S THESIS
The Buddhist no-self (anātmavāda): consciousness is a self-luminous series of
instantaneous cognitions with no permanent Self — and the Self does not exist because it
is not perceived. The rest of the book refutes this.

## 2. EXTENDED QUOTES (French verbatim + English translation)

### Q1 — The Buddhist thesis (lines 2565–2569)
> "il n'existe aucun Soi (ātman) entendu comme une entité consciente permanente, parce que
> la conscience n'est pas une : elle n'est pas un sujet connaissant (jñātṛ) qui demeurerait
> le même… mais une série de cognitions (jñāna) instantanées."
> **"There exists no Self (ātman) understood as a permanent conscious entity, because
> consciousness is not one: it is not a knowing subject (jñātṛ) that would remain the
> same… but a series of instantaneous cognitions (jñāna)."**

### Q2 — Cognition = manifestation, and act/means/result are one (lines 2633–2636)
> "ces trois choses n'en font qu'une, et c'est cette unique entité que les bouddhistes comme
> les philosophes de la Pratyabhijñā nomment 'cognition' (jñāna)."
> **"these three things make but one, and it is this single entity that Buddhists and the
> philosophers of the Pratyabhijñā alike call 'cognition' (jñāna)."**

**→** The shared ground (with Ch5): cognition is act + means + result at once. This unity
is the seed of the felt (a cognition is not a passive thing).

### Q3 — The non-positional reflexivity (lines 3002–3009)
> "la conscience ne s'y vise donc pas objectivement… mais s'y sait être sur un mode
> parfaitement immédiat ou 'non positionnel'."
> **"consciousness does not there aim at itself objectively… but knows itself to be in a
> perfectly immediate or 'non-positional' mode."**

**→ STAGE 1 CLAIM 1.** The reflexivity thesis (prakāśa–vimarśa). Ratié cites Sartre's
non-thetic consciousness (lines 3030–3043) — the modern statement.

### Q4 — The drśyānupalabdhi (the Self is not perceived) (lines 3190–3211)
> "il n'est pas de possession ni de support des cognitions, aucune entité consciente qui les
> transcende."
> **"there is no possessor nor support of the cognitions, no conscious entity that
> transcends them."**

**→** The Buddhist denies the Self because it is not perceived. This is the position the
Śaiva must refute — the hardest-rival test.

### Q5 — Recognition appears WITHIN the list of cognitions (lines 3061–3070)
> "seules des cognitions se manifestent, sous des formes telles que la manifestation d'un
> pot, le concept de pot, la reconnaissance (pratyabhijñā) du pot, le souvenir du pot…"
> **"only cognitions manifest, in forms such as the manifestation of a pot, the concept of
> a pot, the *recognition* (pratyabhijñā) of a pot, the memory of a pot…"**

**→ THE SEED OF THE THESIS.** Recognition is an *ordinary cognition* (like memory, concept)
— yet the Buddhist treats it as a mere construction. The whole book shows it is the felt
structure of the self.

## 3. IPK LINKS
- **ĪPK 1.2.1–2** (the perception-of-Self argument): two cognitions (singular + concept),
  neither belonging to a permanent seer. The Buddhist pūrvapakṣa.
- **ĪPK 1.2.3–6** (the memory argument, developed in Ch2): how could memory arise without a
  permanent self who was the subject of the perception?
- **svasamvedana** (self-awareness): adopted from Dignāga/Dharmakīrti — the shared
  self-luminosity.

## 4. THESIS LITERATURE-REVIEW VALUE
- **The hardest-rival test**: the reflexive self is *defended*, not assumed. This is the
  scholarly gold for Stage 1.
- **The shared self-luminosity**: both Buddhist and Śaiva hold consciousness is
  self-luminous (svaprakāśa); they diverge on whether that implies a Self. This is the
  precise hinge of the thesis.
- **Recognition as an ordinary cognition** (Q5): recognition is a basic, felt fact — not a
  construction. This undercuts the Buddhist and grounds the paper.

## 4b. ⭐ THE ANTARGAḌU ARGUMENT (the strongest no-self challenge — mined from Ratié Ch1,
lines 4287–4303)

The deepest form of the Buddhist denial is the "useless burden" (antargaḍu) argument — the
trace (saṃskāra) suffices to explain memory, so the Self is an unnecessary posit:
> "yady evam antargaḍunā ko'rthaḥ syāt sthāyinātmanā // … un Soi permanent a pour seul
> résultat une fatigue de la faculté spéculative ! … puisque la totalité des expériences
> pratiques dans l'univers est soutenue par la main que lui tend la trace résiduelle."
> "If so, what is the use of the useless burden of a permanent Self? … a permanent Self has
> for its only result a fatigue of the speculative faculty! … since the totality of
> practical experiences in the universe is sustained by the hand held out to it by the
> residual trace."

Ratié adds the Vaiśeṣika's own account (lines 4243–4250): the bhāvanā (memory trace) is
"the cause of **souvenir (smṛti) AND recognition (pratyabhijñāna)** of objects already
experienced." **Recognition already appears in the rival system as an ordinary byproduct
of the trace** — a crucial fact: recognition is not exotic; it is the ordinary phenomenon
every epistemology must handle. This is the point the Śaiva will exploit in Ch2 (see the
svasamvedana dialectic there).
